# Adversarial Track-D

Track-D is what separates decision-grade research from a confident summary. After the other
tracks point to a leading answer, Track-D assumes that answer is **wrong** and goes looking
for the evidence that would prove it. A recommendation that has never been attacked is not
decision-grade, no matter how well-sourced the supporting case is.

## The stance

Treat the leading recommendation as a hypothesis to be falsified, not defended. The
question is not "what supports entering healthcare?" but "if entering healthcare is the
wrong call, what is the evidence, and where would it show up?" This deliberately
counteracts confirmation bias, which otherwise quietly steers the earlier tracks toward the
answer the researcher already prefers.

## What to attack

- **The load-bearing hypotheses.** Take each hypothesis the recommendation depends on and
  search specifically for disconfirming evidence — failed comparable entries, buyers who
  churned, economics that did not hold elsewhere.
- **The strongest evidence.** Re-examine the sources the leading answer leans on hardest.
  Are they primary or echoed? Recent or stale? Representative or cherry-picked?
- **The base rate.** How often do decisions like this one succeed in general? An optimistic
  inside view that ignores the outside base rate is a classic failure.
- **The adversary.** Who wins if this decision is wrong — a competitor, an incumbent — and
  would they act to make it wrong?

## Output

Track-D produces a short memo: what was attacked, what disconfirming evidence was found,
which load-bearing hypotheses survived the attack and which were weakened, and whether the
leading recommendation still stands. If Track-D materially weakens a load-bearing
hypothesis, the recommendation must change or be explicitly hedged — surviving Track-D is a
release gate for the report, not an optional appendix.
