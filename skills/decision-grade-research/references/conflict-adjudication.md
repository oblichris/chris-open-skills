# Conflict Adjudication

Real research surfaces sources that disagree. The weak move is to average them or quietly
pick the convenient one. Decision-grade research instead **adjudicates**: it surfaces the
conflict, weighs the sources, and makes an explicit, recorded call — so a reader can see not
just the conclusion but why one source was trusted over another.

## When to open a conflict memo

Open one whenever two sources give materially different answers to the same load-bearing
hypothesis — different market sizes, opposite churn findings, contradictory regulatory
readings. Trivial disagreements on non-load-bearing points are logged, not adjudicated.

## How to adjudicate

For each conflict, record:

1. **The claim in dispute** and the hypothesis it affects.
2. **Each position** with its source, date, and method.
3. **Source-quality weighing.** Prefer primary over secondary; recent over stale; large or
   representative samples over anecdote; disclosed methodology over a bare number; and
   independent over conflicted (a vendor sizing its own market is suspect).
4. **The resolution.** State which position is adopted and why, or declare the conflict
   unresolved and widen the uncertainty on that hypothesis.

## Honesty rules

- Never silently average two numbers into a false middle; if you blend, say so and justify it.
- Never drop the losing source — record it, so the decision can be revisited if it later
  proves right.
- If the conflict cannot be resolved with available evidence, that is itself a finding:
  flag the hypothesis as `estimated` or `assumed`, widen its uncertainty, and note what new
  evidence would settle it.

The resolved conflicts feed the evidence ledger and the final report's risk section. An
unresolved conflict on a load-bearing hypothesis is a reason to hedge the recommendation or
recommend a cheap test before committing.
